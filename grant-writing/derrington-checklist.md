# Derrington's framework as a checklist

This checklist restates, in its own words, the method Parker Derrington teaches in his workshops and explains in the articles of his catalogue (https://parkerderrington.com/catalogue/): the statement of the case, the three-layer structure, the importance proposition, the summary, the style rules and the five-minute self-review. His articles are the source, and the place to read the method as he puts it; this file only turns it into tests. Everything here is a test to run on text the applicant has written. None of it is a writing order, a sentence template or a paragraph plan, and the model does not propose one. Where the applicant's text fails a test, report the failure and let the applicant decide the repair. Where a funded precedent for the scheme disagrees with a test, the precedent wins and the disagreement is recorded.

The sections say which step of the skill runs them.

## The reader

Panels read many applications in little time. Most readers will not read everything. The document has to give the same picture to someone who reads the summary only, to someone who skims the first page and to someone who reads the whole. If the reader misunderstands, the writer failed.

## 1. Statement of the case (Step 2, and again on the cut text at Step 7)

Derrington's method puts ten sentences at the front of a case. For this checklist they name six things the applicant's statement must contain, in whatever shape and order the applicant chose. Report any that is missing:

- What the whole project will achieve, in one sentence a non-expert can follow, with enough method to be credible and a claim to novelty and ownership.
- Why that matters to this funder, with a number or a citation behind it.
- Each aim, with why it matters.
- What the project as a whole will do.
- For each aim, the work that delivers it.
- What follows: who uses the results, or which field moves and which questions open.

Then three matching tests. Report any that fails:

- Problem and project name the same thing. The question answered is the question asked.
- Each aim and the work that delivers it use the same words. The reader matches them by sight; synonyms break the match.
- The importance claim has a number or a citation behind it.

For a programme, the problem items are the shared questions and each strand has its own project and delivery items. For a nomination against selection criteria, the test is that each criterion has an item and the evidence for it sits where the scheme expects that criterion.

## 2. Structure (Step 3, on the skeleton)

Report any of these that fails:

- The background addresses outcomes in the same order as the work plan describes the work. Mismatched order is the commonest structural failure.
- Each subsection's first sentence states the subsection's point.
- Every direct outcome (knowledge, health, economic, societal) is established in the background with its citation and the funder priority it meets. Indirect outcomes (training, capacity) appear where the form asks for them.
- The form's instructions are followed exactly, however odd.

## 3. Summary consistency (Step 6)

The committee member presenting the application has about five minutes. Report any of these that fails:

- The summary gives the problem and the approach, why it matters, the aims, how the work meets them, and the applications.
- The introduction gives the same picture as the summary, and the whole case is the same skeleton with evidence.
- No number, single study or anecdote appears in the summary that the case does not develop.

## 4. Style (Step 5, and loaded into the checker)

These rules are the applicant-independent part of the checker. Report every sentence or paragraph that fails:

- No sentence over 30 words or with more than one main verb.
- One term per concept, repeated verbatim.
- No coined abbreviations; no shrunk margins or fonts; whitespace kept.
- Active voice by default.

## 5. Paragraph targets (Step 5; untested in the measured projects)

Report every paragraph that misses a target, and let the applicant decide: topic sentence on one line; under about 150 words or ten sentences; about six paragraphs a page; the first sentence asserts and the rest justify; the last sentence joins the next paragraph. Countable because the source is one sentence per line. Applicants have approved paragraphs three times this length. The targets are reported and never imposed.

## 6. The five-minute test (Step 3 on the skeleton; Step 5 with an outside reader)

A fixed prompt for a reader who sees only the summary and the first page (or, at Step 3, only the skeleton), with five minutes:

1. In one sentence, what will this project achieve, and how?
2. Why does it matter, and to whom?
3. What are the aims? Where did you find them?
4. How does the work meet each aim? Did you find the matching sentence for each?
5. How are the results used or shared?
6. Which sentence did you have to read twice?

A question the reader cannot answer is a failure of the text. Report failures only.
